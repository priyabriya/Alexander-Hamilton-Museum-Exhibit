Museum Topic

The exhibit centers on Alexander Hamilton as a case study in the power of the written word. Rather than covering his life as a general biography, it uses a specific lens: Hamilton as a writer whose pen shaped institutions. The through-line runs from the Hurricane Letter of 1772 — the letter that launched his education — through his 51 Federalist Papers, his Report on Public Credit, and ultimately to The Grange, his only home, as a physical artifact of his later life. The argument the exhibit makes is that Hamilton's defining weapon was language, and that his influence persists concretely in the dollar bill, the stock exchange, and the constitutional framework Americans still live under today.

Design Style

The site uses a restrained, archival aesthetic that signals authority and seriousness without feeling academic or dry. Dark backgrounds, serif typography, and deliberate use of negative space create a "primary source document" atmosphere — the visual equivalent of holding something old and significant. Navigation is minimal and linear, guiding visitors through the exhibit like a physical gallery walk: About → Artifacts → Timeline → Audience. The three-panel stat block (51 Federalist Papers, 1790 Report on Public Credit, $10 bill) gives the homepage an immediate orienting "did you know?" quality. The tone is muscular and precise, matching Hamilton's own prose style.

Archetype

The exhibit channels the Sage archetype — authoritative, knowledge-driven, and built around the idea that understanding history is transformative. It doesn't sensationalize Hamilton or lean on the musical's celebrity. Instead, it treats visitors as intelligent adults who want to understand the why behind the founding era. The curatorial voice is that of a knowledgeable guide who has read the primary sources and wants you to read them too, hence the repeated linking directly to the National Archives, the Library of Congress, and the NPS.

Cialdini Principle

The primary Cialdini principle at work is Authority. The exhibit establishes credibility through citation of primary sources (National Archives, Library of Congress, NPS, Wikipedia for overview context), precise historical specificity (disputed birth years, exact dates, exact paper counts), and a curatorial voice that doesn't hedge or speculate loosely. The secondary principle is Social Proof, deployed through the Audience section's three entry points — students, musical fans, and financial systems designers — which signals that many different kinds of people have found Hamilton worth knowing. This lowers the barrier for any individual visitor to feel like they belong in the exhibit.

AI Orchestration Process

The site was built using an AI-assisted workflow in which Claude acted as a multi-role collaborator across the project lifecycle. Rather than treating AI as a single-pass content generator, the process used distinct "modes":

- Researcher: Claude was prompted to surface the most historically significant artifacts and moments in Hamilton's life, filtered through the lens of writing and institutional design — not simply "important things Hamilton did."
- Curator: A second pass used Claude to select and frame four artifacts (Hurricane Letter, Report on Public Credit, The Grange, Federalist Papers) that together tell a coherent narrative arc, rather than a comprehensive list.
- Copywriter: Claude drafted exhibit copy calibrated to a specific reading level and tone — muscular, specific, no filler — and iterated on the homepage hero text until the phrase "built a nation with words" emerged as the organizing idea.
- Critic: Claude was asked to review the drafted site as if it were a museum visitor who arrived skeptical, identifying thin sections, unsupported claims, and missing context.

This orchestration — researcher → curator → copywriter → critic — produced a more coherent exhibit than a single prompt would have.

How the Site Was Planned, Built, Reviewed, and Improved

Planning began with a topic constraint: the exhibit had to be focused enough to be interesting but broad enough to sustain multiple pages. Hamilton was chosen because he offered a natural theme (the pen) that could organize a selective rather than exhaustive treatment. The three audience personas — students, musical fans, financial systems designers — were identified early as a way to give the site multiple entry points without losing focus.
Building followed a content-first approach: copy and structure were drafted before visual design decisions were made. The page hierarchy (Home → Artifacts → Timeline → Audience) was determined by what a first-time visitor would need in sequence: who is this person, what did he leave behind, when did it happen, and why should I care.
Review involved reading the site as three different visitors — the high school student who just saw the musical, the history teacher building a curriculum, and the person who clicked a link without context. Each persona surfaced gaps: the student needed the Hurricane Letter called out as Hamilton's "origin scene"; the teacher needed direct links to primary sources; the context-free visitor needed the stat block up front to orient them quickly.
Improvement focused on two areas: sharpening the curatorial voice so it didn't sound like Wikipedia summary prose, and making the Audience page genuinely useful rather than decorative. The three "Ways In" section was the result — each entry point links directly to the artifact most relevant to that visitor type, so the site functions as a branching exhibit rather than a single prescribed path.
